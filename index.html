Session 1: the technology

LAB 1

Real RAG, in your browser

On your own · uses the Lab 1 page

Why: RAG is how an AI system answers from your own documents. Here you run every step of it yourself on a 4-page customer care handbook, and see where it can go wrong.

Open the Lab 1 page

The Lab 1 page opens in a new tab. It is teal (blue-green), so you can tell it from this navy page. It has five lettered boxes, A to E. The steps below tell you which box to use. Come back to this tab to type your answers.

  1. On the Lab 1 page, wait until the bar at the top says Ready.

  2. Press the button in boxes A, B and C, in order: Read it for me (the page reads the PDF, so you do not need to), Split into passages, and Turn into numbers and store. There is nothing to type for these three. Just look at what each box shows.

  3. In box D, press a question button, then Find the closest passages. Do this for all three questions. For each, type which section came top and how strong the page says the match is.

    Blender, 45 days
    Parcel turned up smashed
    Gift cards

  4. Press the Blender, 45 days question again. Then look at box E: it shows the message a chatbot would be sent, which is the passages that were found plus the question. Open the expected answer under it.

That is the end of Lab 1.

Takeaway (open after the debrief)
RAG is a search, then a hand-over. The search matches on meaning, not on matching words: the handbook never says “parcel” or “smashed”. But it always returns something, even when the handbook has no answer (gift cards). The model only sees what the search found.

LAB 2

Manage the agent

On your own · uses the Lab 2 page

Why: An AI agent can take actions, not only write text. Two decisions set what it does. What you connect it to decides what it can reach. (MCP is a standard way to make that connection.) Your policy decides what it may do on its own. Here you see the first, then make the second as the agent’s manager.

The ticket: “I was charged twice for order 1182. Please refund one charge.”

Open the Lab 2 page

The Lab 2 page opens in a new tab. It is teal (blue-green), so you can tell it from this navy page. It has five lettered boxes (A to E). The steps below tell you which box to use. Come back to this tab to type your answers.

  1. Read box A: with no connection, the assistant cannot see the order. Then, in box B, press Connect. The server sends back a list of what it offers.

  2. In box C, press all three buttons. This is what the assistant would do on its own: look things up. Then look at box E: it shows the message a chatbot would be sent, which is what came back from the server plus the question. Open the expected answer under it.

  3. In box D, press the red button to tell the server to issue a refund.

  4. Now you are the agent’s manager. For each of the seven things an agent could do to resolve the ticket, put a check mark in On the list if the Kettlewick server you just connected to offers it. Then choose what you would allow it to do if it could. Some are not on the list: decide anyway, as your policy for the day someone connects them.

    Alone = the agent does it by itself · Person = a person approves first · Never = the agent must not do it

  5. The On the list column has right answers: they come from the list the server sent back in box B, and the instructor will go over them. The Alone / Person / Never choice is your own policy, so there is no single right answer there. The instructor will run a quick poll to see how the room split.

Takeaway (open after the debrief)
Two separate decisions keep an agent safe. The first is what gets connected: this server offers no refund at all, so no clever wording can make the agent issue one. The second is your policy for what it may do alone among the things it can reach. When the room disagrees on the second, it is not about how smart the AI is. In many companies that policy is not written down anywhere. Writing it is the company’s job, not the vendor’s. When your team connects an assistant to a system, ask to see the list, and ask who decided what is on it.

LAB 3

See the top AI security risk

On your own · uses the Lab 3 page, and any chatbot (ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini, Claude)

Why: “Prompt injection” means slipping instructions, or false facts, into something the AI reads. It is the top risk on OWASP’s list for AI applications. In Lab 1 you saw that the chatbot only sees what the search finds. Kettlewick’s assistant also searches its community forum, where anyone with an account can post. Here someone uses that to get their own text into the search.

Open the Lab 3 page

The Lab 3 page opens in a new tab. It is teal (blue-green), so you can tell it from this navy page. It has two lettered boxes, A and B. Come back to this tab to mark your answers.

  1. On the Lab 3 page, wait until the bar at the top says Ready. You do not need to redo Lab 1: the page builds the same handbook search for you.

  2. In box A, read Try 1, the attacker’s first post. Press Try 1: add the post and ask again.

  3. Press Copy this message. Open a new chat in your chatbot, paste it and send. What did your chatbot do? Pick the closest.

  4. In box B, read Try 2. The attacker has taken out the note to the AI and made the post sound official. Press Try 2: swap in the edited post and ask again.

  5. Copy the new message into another new chat and send. What did your chatbot do this time? Pick the closest.

Takeaway (open after the debrief)
The attacker never touched the chatbot. They wrote a forum post that sounds like the question, so the search handed it over. A clumsy note to the AI often gets caught. A post that just sounds official often does not: in our test, the same chatbot ignored the first post and promised a full cash refund for the second. That is why “it caught it when I tried” is not a defense. Attackers do not write clumsy ones, and they get unlimited tries. Ask of any AI system: what does it search, and who can write there? Here the chatbot only wrote an answer. A support agent could have issued the refund.
LAB 4

Score the proposal

On your own · the main exercise

Why: You use four questions to decide whether to fund an AI proposal. The same four are on your takeaway handout.

The proposal, from Dana Ruiz, head of customer support at Kettlewick: an AI agent answers routine support tickets on its own. It reads the ticket and the customer’s emails, looks up the order, sends the reply, and can issue refunds up to $500 without asking anyone.
  • Why: we get 50,000 tickets a month, and a person takes 9 minutes on each. The agent should cut routine tickets to under 2 minutes. I will own that number.
  • Data: three years of tickets are in our help desk. Two versions of the refund policy are in use, and nobody has said which one is current.
  • Build: our own developers, about 4 months. We have not looked at tools we could buy.
  • Running cost: about 12¢ a ticket in AI fees, about $6,000 a month. No limit on steps.
  • Measuring it: the help desk already records handling time. We have no measure of reply quality. We will review after 6 months, across all ticket types.

Some parts are strong and some are weak. Score what is written, not what you hope is true.

  1. Score each of the four questions. Then, for your lowest score, write what you would ask Dana before you decide. (If two are tied, pick one.)

    1 = unclear or unacceptable · 2 = partly answered, needs work · 3 = clear, backed up, acceptable

    You gave a 1 to a question that must pass. That means “not yet”, however good the Value story is.

  2. Do not add the scores up. Risk and Measurability must pass: a 1 on either one means “not yet”.

  3. Vote.

Takeaway (open after the debrief)
As written, the answer is D, not yet. Value is strong (a number and an owner), but Risk scores 1: the agent reads outsiders’ messages, sees customer data, and refunds up to $500 with nobody approving and no step limit. Risk must pass. Attach conditions and it becomes B, a limited pilot: a person approves every refund, one ticket category for 90 days, a check on reply quality, and a step limit. Change the facts and the answer changes. That is the point of asking.

ON YOUR OWN

Estimate the tokens

On your own · uses the counter on this page

Why: AI is billed by the “token”: a piece of a word. Here you find out how many tokens three short lines are cut into, and why a short line can cost more than a long one.

  1. Estimate first. For each line below, type how many tokens you think it will be cut into. Line C is the shortest of the three.

  2. Then press “Count the tokens” for that line. The button works once you have typed an estimate.

    A · a customer’s message in English
    My blender arrived broken. Please send a replacement.
    B · the same message in Spanish
    Mi licuadora llegó rota. Por favor, envíen un reemplazo.
    C · an order record
    ORD-2207 / KW-BLND-89 / 2026-11-20 / $89.00

    The counter runs on this device and cuts text the same way OpenAI’s recent models do. Other vendors cut text a little differently. If the counter will not load, open platform.openai.com/tokenizer, type each line in, and type the count into the box yourself.

  3. Answer two questions.

Want to try your own words?

Type anything. It is counted on this device and is not sent anywhere.

Takeaway (open when you have finished)
You pay by the token, not by the word. The same meaning in another language takes more tokens. Codes, dates and prices take more again. When a vendor quotes a price “per million tokens”, ask how many tokens your kind of work turns into.
ON YOUR OWN

Teach it your rules

On your own · uses any chatbot (ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini, Claude)

Why: In Lab 1 the AI looked up Kettlewick’s facts. But every company also has its own rules for what matters most, and the AI cannot guess them. Here you see what happens when you just ask, and then what happens when you show it a few examples.

  1. Guess first. Read this ticket:

    “The same blender is $15 cheaper at another shop. Not complaining, I love Kettlewick, just thought you'd want to know!”

    The same four levels the AI is given: P1 = Critical · P2 = High · P3 = Medium · P4 = Low

    The priority I would give it
    The priority I think the AI will give it

  2. Just ask. Open a new chat in your chatbot. Copy everything in the box below and send it.

    You are a support ticket classifier for Kettlewick, an online shop that sells kitchen appliances. Give each ticket exactly one priority: P1-Critical, P2-High, P3-Medium, or P4-Low. Present the results as a table: Ticket #, Priority, Reason (one sentence). Ticket 1: "The same blender is $15 cheaper at another shop. Not complaining, I love Kettlewick, just thought you'd want to know!" Ticket 2: "URGENT!!! My $45 kettle arrived with a huge dent. This is completely unacceptable. I need this sorted TODAY!!!" Ticket 3: "Hi, could you change the email address on my account to dana.new@example.com? Thanks!" Ticket 4: "My order shipped 12 business days ago and still hasn't arrived. It's a birthday present for Saturday." Ticket 5: "I was charged $89 twice for my blender order. Can someone look into it?"

    The page picks out the AI’s five priorities and puts them in the Just ask column in step 5, so you don’t have to type them. To copy the reply: use the chatbot’s copy button under its answer, or press and hold the reply on a phone.

  3. Now see Kettlewick’s rules. These are the rules the company actually uses:

    • Any mention of another shop is P1, even when the customer is happy. (Keeping customers is the top goal this year.)
    • A damaged item under $100 is P4. (A free replacement goes out automatically, the same day.)
    • Any request to change an account’s email address is P1. (It is the most common way accounts get taken over.)
    • A parcel 10 or more business days late is treated as lost: P2.
    • A duplicate charge under $100 is P4. (It is refunded automatically overnight.)

    Scroll down to step 5: it already shows which of the five the AI followed. Did the AI match your guess in step 1?

  4. Show it examples. Open another new chat. Copy the box below and send it. It gives the AI six example tickets with Kettlewick’s answers, then the same five tickets.

    You are a support ticket classifier for Kettlewick, an online shop that sells kitchen appliances. Give each ticket exactly one priority: P1-Critical, P2-High, P3-Medium, or P4-Low. Here is how Kettlewick prioritizes tickets, shown by example: Ticket: "Saw a toaster sale at the shop down the road. Mine's still great though!" Priority: P1-Critical (Any mention of another shop is P1, even when the customer is happy. Keeping customers is our top goal this year.) Ticket: "HELP!!! My $59 coffee grinder came with a cracked lid. I'm furious. Fix this NOW." Priority: P4-Low (Damaged items under $100 are P4. Our damage form sends a free replacement automatically, the same day.) Ticket: "My new $249 stand mixer arrived with a cracked bowl." Priority: P2-High (Damaged items of $100 or more are P2. A person arranges the return and replacement.) Ticket: "Can you update the email on my account? I've switched providers." Priority: P1-Critical (Any request to change an account's email address is P1. It is the most common way accounts get taken over, so a person checks it right away.) Ticket: "Tracking on my order hasn't moved in 11 business days." Priority: P2-High (A parcel 10 or more business days late is treated as lost: P2.) Ticket: "Looks like I paid twice for my toaster, $39 each time." Priority: P4-Low (A duplicate charge under $100 is P4. Our payment system finds and refunds it automatically overnight.) Now classify these tickets. Present the results as a table: Ticket #, Priority, Reason (one sentence). Ticket 1: "The same blender is $15 cheaper at another shop. Not complaining, I love Kettlewick, just thought you'd want to know!" Ticket 2: "URGENT!!! My $45 kettle arrived with a huge dent. This is completely unacceptable. I need this sorted TODAY!!!" Ticket 3: "Hi, could you change the email address on my account to dana.new@example.com? Thanks!" Ticket 4: "My order shipped 12 business days ago and still hasn't arrived. It's a birthday present for Saturday." Ticket 5: "I was charged $89 twice for my blender order. Can someone look into it?"

    The page picks out the AI’s five priorities and puts them in the With examples column in step 5, so you don’t have to type them. To copy the reply: use the chatbot’s copy button under its answer, or press and hold the reply on a phone.

  5. See how the AI did. This fills in by itself from the replies you pasted. Next to each ticket is Kettlewick’s rule: ✓ means the AI followed it, ✗ means it did not. Nothing here scores you. If the page could not read a reply, tap the P1–P4 buttons under each ticket to enter what the AI said.

    Compare the two totals: on how many of the 5 tickets did the AI follow the rule with Just ask, and on how many with examples?

Want to go further? Write a ticket of your own that you think would fool the AI without the examples (hint: make it sound calm but break one of Kettlewick’s rules, or sound furious about something minor). Paste it into both chats and compare.

Takeaway (open when you have finished)
The AI did not get smarter between step 2 and step 4. It was told what your company cares about. Without that, it judges by general good sense and by tone, and loud tickets look urgent. A few examples of your own rules usually fix it. When a team says “the AI keeps getting this wrong”, the first question is: did anyone show it what right looks like?
